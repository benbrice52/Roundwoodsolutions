Grounds Maintenance for Care Homes in Kent: A Guide for Managers and Owners

← Back to blog
November 2026 By Roundwood Solutions Ltd 6 min read

For many care home residents, the garden is the main way they get outside. It is also one of the first things families see when they visit, or when they are choosing a home for a relative. Keeping it safe, tidy and easy to use is part of looking after residents, not just the building.

Roundwood Solutions provides grounds maintenance and landscaping for commercial sites across Kent and Medway, including schools, where safeguarding and working around a daily routine matter just as much. This guide covers what makes care home grounds different, what a grounds maintenance contract should include, and how the work can fit around the home's routine.

What Makes Care Home Grounds Different

Most commercial grounds are looked at more than they are used. Care home gardens are used every day by people who may be frail, unsteady on their feet, using a wheelchair or walking frame, or living with dementia. That changes the priorities:

  • Safe paths — level, even surfaces with no loose slabs or lifted edges, kept clear of leaves, moss and trailing plants. Falls are one of the biggest risks for older people, and an uneven path is easy to miss until someone trips.
  • Calm working — noisy machinery near lounges and bedroom windows can be unsettling, especially for residents living with dementia. Work needs to be timed and planned with the home.
  • Planting that is safe to be near — plants that are poisonous, thorny or sharp-edged are best kept away from paths and seating, or replaced over time.
  • Gardens people can use — seating in both sun and shade, raised beds at a comfortable height for residents who enjoy gardening, and clear routes that are easy to follow.

CQC and the Outdoor Space

CQC's fundamental standards require a care home's premises to be clean, secure, suitable for the way they are used and properly maintained (Regulation 15), and inspectors look at whether residents have access to gardens and other outdoor spaces. A garden with broken paving or overgrown paths can count against a home in the same way as a problem indoors.

A contractor who reports issues promptly, with photos, gives the manager a record that the outside areas are being looked after, and an early warning before something becomes a hazard.

What a Care Home Grounds Contract Should Cover

The basics are the same as any well-run commercial grounds maintenance contract, with the focus on safety and on residents actually using the space:

  • Grass cutting and edging — through the growing season, with clippings cleared from paths so they don't become slippery. Our guide to commercial grass cutting contracts covers frequencies in more detail.
  • Path and patio care — leaves cleared through the autumn, weeds and moss kept off paving, and any loose or lifted slabs reported straight away.
  • Shrubs and hedges — pruned back from paths, windows and seating, keeping clear sightlines for staff supervising residents outside. Main hedge cuts are best planned for autumn and winter, because active birds' nests are protected by law.
  • Weed control — carried out by PA1 and PA6A certified operatives, using no more treatment than needed, timed for when residents are not using the area, and with the area kept clear while the treatment goes down.
  • Seasonal colour — planters and beds near entrances and windows refreshed, so residents who spend more time indoors still have something to look out on.
  • Raised beds and planters — kept planted and tidy for residents who like to garden.

If your home supports residents living with dementia, contrast matters more than colour. Paving in one consistent colour, with seating in a contrasting colour or material, is easier to make sense of than busy patterns. Poisonous, thorny or sharp-edged plants should be kept out of easy reach, and paths kept clear of trailing plants and fallen leaves. A grounds team can help with these changes gradually, as plants and surfaces are replaced.

Working Around the Home's Routine

Every home has its own rhythm, and good grounds work fits around it. Before a contract starts, it's worth agreeing:

  • Times to avoid — mealtimes, quieter rest periods and the times residents usually use the garden. Noisier jobs such as mowing and leaf blowing are best done when the garden is empty.
  • Signing in and out — with reception or the nurse in charge, so staff always know who is on site.
  • Staff checks — DBS-checked staff are available for care settings.
  • One point of contact — so the manager has one person to call about anything outside.

Small Improvements That Make a Difference

Beyond regular maintenance, some of the most useful work in a care home garden is small-scale: ramps and handrails where there are changes in level, relaying uneven paths, raised planters at wheelchair height and new seating areas. We carry out this kind of work as part of our commercial landscaping service, and it can often be planned in alongside regular maintenance.
