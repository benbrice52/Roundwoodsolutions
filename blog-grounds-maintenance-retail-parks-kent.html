Grounds Maintenance for Retail Parks in Kent: A Guide for Property and Asset Managers

← Back to blog
November 2026 By Roundwood Solutions Ltd 6 min read

A retail park is judged from the car. Before a shopper reaches a store, they have driven past the entrance planting, parked between landscaped islands and walked along the shop frontages. If those areas look neglected, it reflects on every tenant on the park, and it is usually the managing agent who hears about it first.

Roundwood Solutions provides grounds maintenance for commercial sites across Kent and Medway, working with property managers, managing agents and FM companies. This guide covers what makes retail parks different from other commercial sites, what a grounds maintenance contract for a retail park should include, and how to plan the work around trading hours.

Why Retail Parks Need a Different Approach

Most commercial sites have quiet periods. A retail park is open to the public from early morning into the evening, seven days a week, and its busiest days are the ones when offices and industrial estates are empty. Grounds work has to fit around shoppers, delivery vehicles and a car park that is rarely empty.

The landscaping is also spread across the site in a way that makes it harder to manage than a single office frontage:

  • Car park islands and planters dotted across the parking areas, often with trees, shrubs and lighting columns sharing the same small bed.
  • Entrance roads, roundabouts and verges that set the first impression and need to stay low enough for drivers to see clearly.
  • Beds and planters outside the units, where every shopper walks past the planting at close range.
  • Boundary hedges and banks that catch litter blown across the car park.

Then there is the litter itself. Fast food outlets, coffee drive-throughs and trolley bays all add to it, and most of it ends up in the planting.

What a Retail Park Grounds Contract Should Cover

The basics are the same as any well-run commercial grounds maintenance contract, but the priorities shift. A good specification for a retail park will usually include:

  • Litter picking before every visit — beds, verges and hedge bases cleared before any mowing or strimming starts. Cutting over litter shreds it into hundreds of small pieces that are far harder to clear.
  • Car park islands and planters — shrubs pruned to stay clear of parking bays, lighting columns, CCTV cameras and tenant signage, and kept low near pedestrian crossings so drivers can see people stepping out.
  • Grass cutting and edging — verges, entrance banks and roundabouts cut regularly through the growing season. Our guide to commercial grass cutting contracts covers frequencies in more detail.
  • Weed control on hard surfaces — kerb lines, block paving, footpaths and the edges of parking bays, treated by PA1 and PA6A certified operatives at quieter times, with the area kept clear of the public while the treatment goes down.
  • Hedge and boundary cutting — main cuts planned for autumn and winter, because active birds' nests are protected by law. Any lighter trims in spring and summer should only go ahead after checking for nesting birds.
  • Leaf clearance and drains — wet leaves on footpaths are a slip risk, and leaves washed into gullies are a common cause of standing water in car parks. Clearing both through the autumn should be part of the contract, not an extra.
  • Seasonal planting — entrance planters and feature beds refreshed so the park looks cared for where shoppers notice it most.

Many retail parks were built with a landscaping scheme approved as part of the planning permission, and the planning conditions commonly require any planting that dies to be replaced for five years after completion. Even outside that period, dead shrubs in car park islands are easy to miss from the management office but obvious to every shopper who parks next to them. Autumn and winter are the best time to replace them, while plants are dormant and have time to establish before summer.

Working Around Trading Hours

The biggest difference between a retail park and other commercial sites is timing. Mowing next to a busy store entrance, or running a leaf blower along the shop fronts at midday, puts noise and debris right where customers are walking. A contractor who hasn't planned for that will cause complaints even if the work itself is done well.

Before a contract starts, it's worth agreeing:

  • Visit times — noisier work near entrances and shop fronts done early, before stores open, with quieter jobs such as pruning and hand weeding later in the morning.
  • How car park areas are worked — small sections coned off at a time rather than whole rows of bays, with disabled and parent-and-child spaces kept open.
  • Peak trading periods — the run-up to Christmas, Black Friday weekend and bank holidays. Most retail parks want leaf clearance, pruning and any larger jobs finished before late November, with only light visits through December.
  • Who the team reports to on site — signing in and out with the management office or security, and one named contact on the contractor's side who deals with any issues.

Service Charges and Keeping Tenants Informed

On most retail parks, grounds maintenance is paid for through the service charge, so tenants will want to know what they are paying for. The easier it is to show what was done and when, the easier the managing agent's job becomes.

That starts with a written specification: what is done on each visit, how often, and how additional works are priced and approved. Wording like "maintain to a reasonable standard" with nothing behind it leads to disputes. After each visit, a short report with photos gives the managing agent something to share with tenants. Anything spotted on site, such as damaged bollards, fly-tipping or lifted paving, can be reported with a photo the same day.

Insurance and Compliance

Any contractor working on a site open to the public should hold at least £5M public liability insurance and provide risk assessments and method statements before work starts. Herbicide treatments must only be carried out by operatives holding valid PA1 and PA6A certificates. That is a legal requirement, and a retail park, with the public walking past every treated surface, is not the place to get it wrong.
